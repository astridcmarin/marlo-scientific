How the curve is built

One compartment with first-order absorption, typical values, apparent parameters (divided by the unknown bioavailability):

  • CL/F {{ fmt2|format(v.parameters.cl_f_l_h) }} L/h — {{ v.parameters.provenance.cl_f_l_h }}
  • V/F {{ fmt1|format(v.parameters.v_f_l) }} L — {{ v.parameters.provenance.v_f_l }}
  • ka {{ fmt2|format(v.parameters.ka_per_h) }} per h — {{ v.parameters.ka_rule }}

Why not the label's headline clearance? The label also gives CL/F 7–10 L/h and V/F 62–138 L, pooled across myeloma and Kaposi sarcoma. A curve from those numbers predicts {{ fmt0|format(v.checks.consistency.rejected_set.B_stated_pooled.auc) }} ng·h/mL and a peak of {{ fmt0|format(v.checks.consistency.rejected_set.B_stated_pooled.cmax) }} ng/mL — about half of what the same label reports for myeloma patients — and fails the check below.

Weakest point: the peak. The label curve peaks earlier and about 20% higher than Li's typical curve, because its absorption rate comes from the label's Tmax. Taking Tmax as 2 h would raise the peak ratio to {{ fmt2|format(v.checks.outside.ka_sensitivity[0].cmax_ratio_to_li) }}, just outside the band. Daily exposure and trough are robust.

Li 2017 reports a fitted model and simulated exposures, not observed concentrations, so this is a comparison of two models — weaker than agreement with data — and both come from the manufacturer's programme.
